About this site

cammap is a map of the facial recognition cameras in London that the public record supports: the places the Metropolitan Police has sent its live facial recognition vans, its fixed cameras in Croydon, the stations the British Transport Police has run it at, and the shops that have said they use it. Nothing on it is estimated. This page says what the cameras are, where every entry comes from, what the map does not claim, and who is behind it.

What live facial recognition is

A CCTV camera records what happens in front of it for someone to look at later. A live facial recognition camera looks at every face that passes it, as it passes: it measures the face, turns the measurements into a biometric template, compares that template against a watchlist of people the operator is looking for, and raises an alert when the score is high enough. Your face becomes a barcode in the same way as your fingerprint or your DNA. The camera does not need to know you to do it; it does it to everyone who walks past.

In London it has come in five forms, and the map colours them apart. The Metropolitan Police runs it from vans parked for a shift and from a pair of fixed cameras in Croydon. The British Transport Police has run it from temporary rigs at stations. Some shops run Facewatch, a private system whose watchlist shop staff add people to, under different law from the police cameras. And one private estate, King's Cross Central, ran its own system on its developer's cameras until an inquiry ended it in 2019; the map keeps it as the one entry of its kind. What each of them means for you when you are standing in front of one is on the rights page.

Where every camera comes from

Every entry on the map comes from a published source: the Met's own deployment records, which list the places its vans were sent and how often, by period; the British Transport Police's deployment register for its station trial; and, for the fixed cameras and the shops, named press reporting and the Met's own announcement of the Croydon cameras. Each camera carries the name of the record or report it rests on and, where there is one, a link to it. Where no source is known the map says nothing rather than something vague.

The record itself is one file of comma-separated values in the repository, one camera per line, and everything the site shows is written out from it by a script: the data the map draws, and the rows the database is filled from. It is edited by hand, from the documents, and nothing fills a blank by guessing - where a source gives no value, the record holds none. The line under the map says how many cameras there are, which years the records run to, and when the record was last checked. The whole record can be taken away as a file, or read over the same interface the map reads it from: the data page says what is in it and how.

What the map does not claim

It does not say that a van is anywhere now. A van parks for a shift and drives away, so there is no hour at which "a van is at this spot" is a thing this map can honestly say. What the record supports is that a van was sent here, this many times, most recently in this year, and that is what a van site's entry says. Every van site is marked legacy for that reason and sits behind the Legacy toggle; the map opens on the cameras that are fixed to something.

It does not predict. Using the record to guess where the vans will go next was considered and dropped: a confident guess printed beside a public record invites people to read it as one. What the map offers instead is the record's own answer to the question people have - the Most used sort, which orders the sites by how many times a source records them being used, and the glow, which is weighed by the same number.

It does not pretend to a precision the sources lack. The Met's records give a van site as a street or a district, never a coordinate, and where a record gives only an area the pin sits at the middle of it and the camera's note says so. A station entry is the station, not the spot on the concourse. And it is not complete: a shop is not required to publish where its cameras are - a sign on the door is the whole of the disclosure - so only the shops that have named their stores are on the map, and a place that is not on the map is not known to be free of one.

Anyone can report a camera, with a photo of it. It goes on the map when a moderator has checked the report, or when enough people have reported the same spot independently.

Who runs this, and how it is paid for

Volunteers. There is no organisation behind it, no office and no names on it - the site puts no names on anyone, and it begins with itself. It is free and carries no advertising, and donations pay for the hosting. An account, for the people who report cameras, is a username of two random words and a password: no email address, no real name, and no IP address kept in any table of ours. The account page says what is kept, and what cannot be taken back once a report has put a camera on the map. Writing about this? The press page has the counts, the method and the terms for reuse, and says how to get in touch.

The licence

The record - every camera on the map - is published under the Open Database License 1.0, so that it can be cited, plotted and built on. The code that draws it is not licensed for reuse. Both are set out in the licence in the repository.
