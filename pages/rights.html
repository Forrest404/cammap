Know your rights

This is not legal advice. It is what the public documents say, with a link to each one, and nothing that is not in one of them. It was checked on 7 September 2026. The law on facial recognition is changing - the last section says how - so look at that date before you rely on anything here, and if you are in trouble, speak to a solicitor.

If a van is in front of you now

You can walk past it, or not. No law says you must walk through the cameras, and the police's own documents are written on the basis that you can see them coming and choose. The Met's policy requires a deployment to be overt: signage "outside of the Zone of Recognition", a marked vehicle, and officers "able to answer public questions" [Met LFR policy, §10]. At stations the trial runs "in signed areas of TfL stations, allowing customers to make informed choices about passing through" [TfL, 10 Aug 2026], and the British Transport Police has said that people who prefer not to enter the zone will be given another route [Biometric Update, 11 Aug 2026]. BTP names its next deployment on its own website before it happens [BTP, facial recognition].

You do not have to show your face to a camera. There is no general duty to. The two powers this page could find that let a police officer require a face covering to be removed both need an authorisation in force for that place and time: section 60AA of the Criminal Justice and Public Order Act 1994, where an inspector believes offences are likely in the area, for up to 24 hours, with failure to comply an offence [CJPOA 1994, s60AA]; and, since 29 June 2026, section 157 of the Crime and Policing Act 2026, in an area an inspector has designated at a protest, with defences for health, religion and work [Crime and Policing Act 2026, s157] [Home Office factsheet]. Neither is about facial recognition, and neither applies unless the authorisation has been given. You can ask an officer whether one is in force.

Covering it can still get you stopped. The one documented case: on 31 January 2019, outside Romford station, a man pulled his jumper up over his face as he passed the Met's van. Officers stopped him; the Met said he "became aggressive and made threats towards officers", and he was given a £90 penalty notice for disorder [Romford Recorder, Jan 2019] [Big Brother Watch]. On the Met's account the penalty was for the exchange with the officers and not for the covered face - which is the point. The stop is where it goes wrong, and staying calm and asking under what power costs nothing.

You can ask what is going on. The policy commits the Met to officers at the deployment who can answer questions, and to offering a leaflet to anyone it stops [Met LFR policy, §10]. Worth asking: is this live facial recognition, and which of the policy's three use cases is it - a crime or missing-person hotspot, a protective security operation, or specific intelligence? Outside those three the policy does not allow it [judgment summary, 21 Apr 2026].

What happens to your face if you are not on the list. The Met's policy says the biometric template of anyone who does not raise an alert is "immediately and automatically deleted", the watchlist is deleted within 24 hours of the deployment ending, and the CCTV footage within 31 days [Met LFR policy, §3 and §9]. The station trial says the same of anyone not matched [TfL, 10 Aug 2026]. That is what they say. There is no way to check it from the street.

If you are stopped

What a match is. The system scores your face against every image on the watchlist and raises an alert when the score passes a threshold - 0.64 in every deployment in the Met's 2025 record and in BTP's register [Met deployment record 2025] [BTP deployment register 2026]. An officer is then meant to compare the two images by hand before anyone is approached; the policy says it "must not be an automatic consequence that an Alert results in an Engagement" [Met LFR policy, §7].

An alert is not grounds for arrest. The Met's policy says an alert "on its own, indicating that a person is on the Watchlist, should not be taken as providing sufficient grounds by itself for arrest, search or detention", and that if a person stopped "cannot be identified or fails to confirm their identity, this alone does not constitute a criminal offence and does not necessarily render them liable to arrest" [Met LFR policy, §7]. Officers asking you questions after an alert are, in the policy's own words, using "common law powers to ask questions" - questions, rather than any power particular to facial recognition.

Wrong matches happen. On 23 February 2024, near London Bridge, Shaun Thompson was matched to an image of his brother, stopped, questioned, asked to prove his identity, and threatened with arrest when he declined to give fingerprints [judgment summary, 21 Apr 2026]. The Met's own annual report for 2025 counts ten false alerts, six of them spoken to, out of about three million faces seen [Met LFR annual report 2025].

Fingerprints on the street. An officer can take your fingerprints without consent, away from a police station, only if they reasonably suspect you of an offence and either do not know your name and cannot readily find it out, or have reasonable grounds to doubt the name you give [PACE 1984, s61(6A)–(6B)]. If you are asked, ask under which power and what offence you are suspected of: the policy above says an alert by itself is not enough.

Write it down. The time, the place, the officers' numbers, what you were told. A stop after an alert makes a record, and the policy keeps an alert's data for up to 31 days, longer where a complaint is being looked into [Met LFR policy, §9] - so if you want a copy, ask soon. The next two sections say how.

Who to complain to

The force first. A complaint about how officers behaved goes to the force's own professional standards department - the Met, or the British Transport Police at a station. The Independent Office for Police Conduct says most complaints are handled by the force, that it investigates the most serious itself, and that you can ask it for a review if you are unhappy with the outcome [IOPC, Complaints]. The Met's own contact for facial recognition, given in its policy, is LFR@met.police.uk [Met LFR policy, §10].

The ICO for your data. How your face was processed - by the police under Part 3 of the Data Protection Act 2018, or by a shop under the UK GDPR - is a data protection matter, and the Information Commissioner's Office takes complaints about it [ICO, Make a complaint]. The ICO audited five police forces' use of facial recognition in 2026 and published what it found [ICO, Aug 2026].

The Commissioner. The Biometrics and Surveillance Camera Commissioner's role is "to encourage compliance with the surveillance camera code of practice", which is the code police forces are bound to have regard to; the office takes enquiries at enquiries@obscc.org.uk [gov.uk].

Not confirmed: whether the post currently has a holder. gov.uk names nobody; the survey this page draws on found it vacant since August 2024 in one place and filled in November 2025 in another. An appointment notice on gov.uk would settle it.

London's own scrutiny. The Mayor's Office for Policing and Crime and the London Assembly's Police and Crime Committee question the Met. A February 2026 report by a member of the Assembly called for a pause in the Met's expansion and for the data the Met withholds [London Assembly, Feb 2026]. Big Brother Watch attends deployments it is tipped off about and takes reports of them by email and Signal [Big Brother Watch]. And this site takes reports of cameras.

Asking what is held on you

You have a right to be told whether an organisation holds personal data about you, and to a copy of it. Anyone can ask, without a solicitor, and the organisation usually has one month [ICO, Getting copies of your information]. For the police the right is section 45 of the Data Protection Act 2018, and the month, extendable by two for a complex request, is section 54 [DPA 2018, s45] [DPA 2018, s54]. For a shop, or Facewatch, it is article 15 of the UK GDPR, free of charge unless the request is "manifestly unfounded or excessive" [UK GDPR, art 15] [UK GDPR, art 12]. The police may withhold what would prejudice an investigation, and must normally tell you that they have [DPA 2018, s45(4)].

What to ask the Met. Put "subject access request" in the subject line, give the date, time and place of the deployment and what happened, and ask for any alert record, images or notes about you, and whether you were on a watchlist. The ICO's page says what else a request should contain [ICO]. If you walked past and nothing happened, expect nothing back: the Met says it deletes non-matches at once [Met LFR policy, §3]. If you were stopped, ask quickly, for the 31-day reason above. The Met's policy says it answers requests made under the Data Protection Act, and gives LFR@met.police.uk for anything about the technology [Met LFR policy, §10].

Shops

A different system, and thinner rules. Facewatch is a private company. It is the data controller, not the shop; the shop's part is to display a sign saying facial recognition is in operation [Facewatch privacy notice]. The notice on a door reads: "Facial recognition in operation. To protect our employees, customers and stock. Our legal process for processing your data is our legitimate interest and the substantial public interest of preventing and detecting crime" [KentOnline, Aug 2023]. That sentence is the legal basis - legitimate interests under the UK GDPR - and the sign is the whole of the disclosure: no authorisation, no time limit, and no register of which shops have it [Facewatch privacy notice].

Who is on the list. Subscribing shops report people they suspect. Facewatch says the biometric data of anyone not on its list is "instantly deleted", that an entry is kept for up to twelve months from the last recorded incident, two years where a weapon was involved, and that an alert can go to other subscribing shops "in a defined area relevant to the subscriber" [Facewatch privacy notice]. In March 2023 the ICO told Facewatch it had breached the UK GDPR on eight counts - among them lawfulness, fairness and transparency, and the processing of biometric and criminal-offence data - accepted the changes it made, and decided that "regulatory action is not required" [ICO closure letter, 28 Mar 2023]. One of those changes, as Big Brother Watch reported it, was to limit the watchlist to repeat offenders or more serious offences [Big Brother Watch, Jun 2023].

If you are wrongly flagged. Twice in 2026 it reached the press in London: a man ejected from Sainsbury's at Elephant and Castle in January, who then had to send Facewatch a copy of his passport and a photograph so that it could confirm he was not on its database [The Register, 6 Feb 2026]; and a man ejected at East Dulwich in August, after which the store's cameras were paused [Retail Gazette, Aug 2026]. If it happens to you: ask the shop what the alert said; write to Facewatch's data protection officer, whose address is in its privacy notice, to see the entry and have it corrected or removed; and if that fails, complain to the ICO [Facewatch privacy notice] [ICO].

The law is changing

There is still no Act of Parliament about facial recognition. Police use rests on common law powers, Part 3 of the Data Protection Act 2018, the Human Rights Act and each force's own policy [judgment summary, 21 Apr 2026]. In 2020 the Court of Appeal held South Wales Police's use unlawful, because its policy left too much discretion over who went on a watchlist and where the cameras went - not because the technology was unlawful in itself [R (Bridges) v South Wales Police [2020] EWCA Civ 1058]. On 21 April 2026 the High Court dismissed a challenge to the Met's 2024 policy, holding that it does constrain that discretion [R (Thompson and Carlo) v Commissioner of Police of the Metropolis [2026] EWHC 915 (Admin), press summary]. It declined to decide whether a policy written for vans that come and go covers cameras fixed to a wall, as the ones in Croydon are [Inforrm, 19 May 2026].

Not confirmed: whether an appeal against that judgment has been lodged or heard. On the day of the judgment Big Brother Watch said it supported Mr Thompson "in seeking to appeal" and was raising funds for it [Big Brother Watch, 21 Apr 2026]. A listing in the Court of Appeal, or a judgment on caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk, would settle it.

The Home Office consulted on a new legal framework for police use of biometrics between 4 December 2025 and 12 February 2026. As of the date at the top of this page the consultation is closed and no response and no bill has been published [Home Office consultation]. Shops are outside that consultation altogether [Statewatch, Feb 2026]. When any of this changes, this page should change with it.
